London · 1815 – 1852

Ada Lovelace

Mathematician, writer, and the first person to publish a program for a machine that had not yet been built.

Biography

A mathematical imagination

Ada was born in London on 10 December 1815, the daughter of the poet Lord Byron and Annabella Milbanke, a woman with a serious interest in mathematics. Her parents separated only weeks after the birth, and she was raised by her mother with no memory of her father, who left England in 1816 and died in 1824. Annabella, wary of the Byron temperament, made sure her daughter's education leaned heavily on arithmetic, logic and science.

That grounding shaped an unusual mind. As a child Ada filled her notebooks with designs, among them a winged flying machine she called "Flyology". As a teenager she was introduced to the scientists of the day. Mary Somerville, the mathematician and astronomer, became a friend and mentor, and Augustus De Morgan later took her through advanced mathematics by letter.

In June 1833, at seventeen, she met Charles Babbage at one of his London gatherings and watched a demonstration of his Difference Engine. The finished machine was not what held her attention. It was the Analytical Engine he was still designing: a general-purpose device with a store, a mill and punched cards borrowed from the Jacquard loom. The two began a correspondence that lasted the rest of her life.

In 1842 she translated Luigi Menabrea's Italian account of the Analytical Engine for Taylor's Scientific Memoirs. Babbage urged her to add notes of her own, and the result — published in 1843 — ran longer than the paper itself. Note G sets out a step-by-step method for calculating Bernoulli numbers, complete with variables, a loop and a table of operations, and is generally treated as the first published computer program. Her real argument was larger: the engine could work on symbols as well as numbers, and might one day handle music or any subject that could be expressed in symbols. She was just as clear about its limits, and warned against the idea that it could think for itself. She had married William King in 1835 and became Countess of Lovelace in 1838; the couple had three children. She died of cancer on 27 November 1852, aged thirty-six.

Key achievements

A life in dates

  1. 1815

    Born in London

    10 December. The only legitimate child of Lord Byron and Annabella Milbanke.

  2. 1828

    A childhood of inventions

    Aged twelve, she designs a flying machine and calls the study of flight "Flyology".

  3. 1833

    Meets Charles Babbage

    Sees the Difference Engine demonstrated and learns about the Analytical Engine he is planning.

  4. 1835

    Marriage

    Marries William King, who becomes Earl of Lovelace in 1838. They have three children.

  5. 1843

    The Notes

    Publishes her translation of Menabrea's paper with notes A to G. Note G holds the Bernoulli-number program.

  6. 1852

    Death

    Dies in London on 27 November, aged thirty-six. Her notes are soon forgotten.

  7. 1953

    Rediscovery

    B. V. Bowden reprints the notes in Faster Than Thought, and her place in computing history is restored.

  8. 1980

    A language named Ada

    The US Department of Defense names its new programming language after her.

In her own words

The Analytical Engine has no pretensions whatever to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform.

Ada Lovelace, Note G, 1843

Alan Turing later called this "Lady Lovelace's objection" in his 1950 paper on machine intelligence.

Legacy

A name on a language

The Analytical Engine was never completed in her lifetime, and for a century her notes were read by very few people. Their reprinting in 1953 changed that. Today Note G is treated as a founding document of computing, and the argument she made about symbols — that a machine might work on more than numbers — reads like a description of the century that followed.

Whether she or Babbage deserves the title "first programmer" is still argued over. What is not in doubt is that hers was the first program published, with its method explained so that other people could follow it.

In her honour

  • 1953 — the notes reprinted in Faster Than Thought
  • 1980 — the Ada language, standardised as MIL-STD-1815 after her birth year
  • 2009 — the first Ada Lovelace Day, held each October
  • Today — the Chalon portrait is held by the Science Museum Group
